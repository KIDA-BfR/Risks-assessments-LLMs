Your description is as follows: Description: From an environmental perspective, the Campylobacter problem is a direct consequence of intensive agricultural practices (Szott & Friese, 2021). This viewpoint frames modern livestock farming as the creator of vast pathogen reservoirs that lead to contamination of the wider ecosystem (Schets et al., 2017). The continuous shedding of Campylobacter establishes environmental transmission cycles that affect water, soil, and wildlife, underscoring the deep interconnectedness of farm management, ecological health, and public health (Gölz et al., 2014).
Intensive agricultural operations — particularly those for poultry, cattle, and pigs — serve as primary reservoirs continuously shedding high concentrations of Campylobacter into the environment (Hermans et al., 2012). The pathogen is dispersed from farms via multiple routes (Szott & Friese, 2021):
●	Manure and Slurry: The application of animal waste as fertilizer, or runoff from storage areas, leads to direct contamination of soil and waterways (Jones, 2001).
●	Airborne Transmission: Pathogens can become aerosolized from dust and particles exhausted from mechanically ventilated animal houses (de Rooij et al., 2019).
●	Water Contamination: Farm runoff is a significant source of Campylobacter in surface waters, including recreational lakes and streams used for irrigation (Brooks et al., 2009).
●	Vector Spread: Insects (especially flies) and rodents act as mechanical vectors, physically carrying the bacteria from farms to other locations (Hald et al., 2004).
Further complicating control is the pathogen’s ability to survive in harsh environmental conditions by entering a “viable but non-culturable” (VBNC) state (Silva et al., 2011). In this dormant-like condition, the bacteria do not grow on standard laboratory media — making them undetectable by conventional methods (Oliver, 2005) — but may remain metabolically active and potentially infectious (Ramamurthy et al., 2014).
These stakeholders also emphasize that any regulatory changes — such as replacing the current Process Hygiene Criterion with a stricter Food Safety Criterion — must be based on sound risk analysis and proportionality. They advocate focusing interventions at the source of contamination (farm and slaughterhouse) in a cost-efficient way, rather than imposing broad, blanket measures that could lead to unnecessary downstream impacts like excessive food waste (Foddai et al., 2024). Environmental NGOs even collaborate with food safety risk managers (including industry) to interpret surveillance data and QMRA findings, highlighting how seasonal trends, model uncertainties, and stage-specific contamination influence outcomes (Foddai et al., 2024). This evidence-driven approach ensures that new criteria yield real public health benefits without undermining sustainability goals.

General aim: To mitigate the environmental transmission of Campylobacter and other zoonotic pathogens through strengthened manure management, enhanced scientific understanding of environmental risks, and the adoption of integrated One Health farming systems — thereby reducing pathogen circulation between animals, humans, and ecosystems and supporting sustainable, biosecure food production. This approach aims to maximize public health gains per unit of control effort by aligning interventions with risk-based performance objectives (FSO/PO framework), ensuring that safety criteria are efficient and do not inadvertently exacerbate food waste or other environmental burdens (Van Schothorst et al., 2009; Nauta & Havelaar, 2008).. Scientific Foundation: The environmental perspective is supported by scientific evidence tracing the pathogen’s journey from the farm into the natural world. Numerous studies have isolated Campylobacter from a wide range of environmental samples, including soil (Jones, 2001), flies (Hald et al., 2004), and recreational water sources (Guy et al., 2018), directly linking agricultural activity to environmental contamination (Bull et al., 2006). Microbiological research on the VBNC state provides the mechanism that explains the pathogen’s unexpected tenacity and persistence outside a host (Oliver, 2005). This focus on environmental pathways is justified by the One Health concept, a globally recognized approach positing that human, animal, and environmental health are inextricably linked (Gölz et al., 2014).
Environmental advocates highlight that food waste itself constitutes a major ecological burden. When poultry products are discarded due to overly stringent criteria, all the embedded greenhouse-gas emissions, land, and water inputs are wasted, aggravating environmental impact. From a One Health and circular-economy perspective, control measures must therefore evaluate life-cycle sustainability, as promoted by the EU Green Deal and SDG. Environmental stakeholders further note that discarded meat carries a hidden environmental footprint. Tightening microbiological limits without harmonized waste-mitigation strategies could increase overall climate impact. They advocate for circular approaches — such as safe reprocessing, rendering, or alternative valorization routes — to minimize the ecological burden of food rejected under stricter criteria.
. Ethical Foundation: Environmental NGOs have an ethical responsibility to safeguard the ecological balance that underpins human and animal health. Intensive livestock farming creates vast Campylobacter reservoirs that contaminate soil, water, and wildlife, turning an agricultural challenge into an environmental and public health issue. Under One Health principles and the Manhattan Principles, NGOs are morally obligated to advocate for sustainable farming systems that prevent pathogen leakage into the environment. This duty is rooted in the principle of ecological stewardship — the obligation to prevent avoidable harm to ecosystems and communities affected by agricultural pollution. Continuous shedding of Campylobacter through manure, runoff, and airborne particles externalizes health risks onto others, violating the ethical norms of fairness and shared responsibility. They further invoke the principle of proportionality: control measures should be guided by surveillance evidence and focused on the most impactful points (e.g., farms and slaughterhouses), rather than broad-brush actions that create undue waste or burden (Foddai et al., 2024).
Accordingly, environmental NGOs must promote science-based manure management, research on environmental persistence, and integrated farm systems that minimize contamination at its source. Their ethical mission is to ensure that food production supports — not undermines — the long-term health of people, animals, and ecosystems.
