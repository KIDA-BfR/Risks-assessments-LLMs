Your description is as follows: Description: From the perspective of food safety authorities, Campylobacter infection represents a major zoonotic threat with profound implications for population health and food safety. It is the most frequently reported bacterial zoonosis in many industrialized countries, with reported incidence rates increasing from 4–5 cases per 100,000 inhabitants in 1997 to 14–15 cases per 100,000 by 2015. However, these figures substantially underestimate the true burden of disease — the actual incidence is estimated to be 7–100 times higher due to widespread underreporting (Backert 2021). While most human cases of campylobacteriosis present as self-limiting gastroenteritis, the infection can result in serious post-infectious complications such as Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS), reactive arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (Abd El-Hack et al., 2021; Backert 2021). Vulnerable groups — including infants, young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals — are particularly susceptible to these severe outcomes (Backert 2021). Moreover, the growing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance among Campylobacter strains further exacerbates the public health challenge, as increasing resistance to fluoroquinolones (second-line treatments) and macrolides (first-line drugs) severely limits therapeutic options and complicates the management of severe infections (Soro et al., 2020).
Given its scale, clinical severity, and socioeconomic impact, Campylobacter infection constitutes a significant and persistent public health crisis. These factors necessitate robust regulatory action and the implementation of systematic, population-wide risk management strategies aimed at reducing pathogen loads throughout the food production and distribution chain. Through the implementation of a Food Safety Criterion (FSC) under Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005 it is assumed that human campylobacteriosis cases attributable to the consumption of poultry meat will decrease, enhancing food safety. Within this EU legal framework (Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005 and its 2017 amendment addressing Campylobacter in broiler meat), food safety authorities also provide scientific advice, set and implement microbiological criteria, and oversee risk-based controls along the poultry meat chain (Foddai et al., 2024). They draw on EFSA’s baseline survey data and scientific opinions, as well as quantitative microbial risk assessment (QMRA) models, to align criteria and targets with public health outcomes at each stage of production and processing (EFSA, 2010a; EFSA, 2010b; EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2011; Foddai et al., 2024).
. Short-term goals: ●	Implement Enforceable Food Safety Criterion (FSC): Based on quantitative risk assessment models, establish and enforce a clear microbiological standard for poultry meat (e.g. for broiler or turkey carcasses or cuts). Since a PHC with a limit of 1,000 colony forming units (CFU) per gram of chicken skin was projected to reduce human cases attributable to poultry by over 60% (Swart et al., 2013) but did not lead to the desired effect (i.e. no significant reduction in human case rates), a food safety criterion according to EU Regulation 2073/2005 (EC, 2005) should be established, being applicable throughout the shelf-life. As part of this process, authorities will evaluate the criterion’s projected public health benefits relative to its economic and operational costs, using surveillance-integrated QMRA analyses (EFSA, 2010a; EFSA, 2010b; EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2011; Foddai et al., 2024) to ensure the new standard is proportionate and truly risk-based.
●	Strengthen Epidemiological Surveillance: Advocate for mandatory, harmonized surveillance systems for both human campylobacteriosis cases and pathogen levels throughout the food chain. This is essential to close data gaps created by voluntary or inconsistent reporting schemes, which currently hinder effective risk assessment and management. In line with a One Health approach, surveillance efforts should integrate data across human, animal, and food sectors and harmonize the use of diagnostic methods and risk assessment tools to support coordinated risk management (Foddai et al., 2024).
●	Promote Rapid Diagnostic Technologies: Support the validation and deployment of rapid, on-site testing methods on farms and in processing plants. Traditional surveillance, such as Norway’s APaC system, can miss flocks that become contaminated in the final week before slaughter due to sample transportation and analysis delays. Rapid technologies close this critical time gap, enabling timely control measures and preventing highly contaminated food from entering the market.
. General aim: To reduce the incidence of Campylobacter infections in humans by implementing risk-based interventions across the poultry production continuum — including enforceable microbiological food safety criteria according to EU Regulation 2073/2005, harmonized epidemiological surveillance, and the adoption of rapid diagnostic technologies — to enable timely detection, control, and prevention of contamination and disease transmission. . Scientific Foundation: Scientific source-attribution studies consistently identify contaminated poultry meat as a primary vehicle for Campylobacter transmission; in the Netherlands, for instance, an estimated 30% of all human cases are attributed to this source. The societal cost is substantial, with an estimated annual economic burden of €2.4 billion in the European Union (Backert 2021) and €82 million in the Netherlands (Swart et al., 2013). The scientific basis for severe complications is also well-documented. GBS, for example, is triggered by molecular mimicry, where lipooligosaccharides (LOS) on the surface of certain Campylobacter strains resemble human gangliosides, causing the immune system to mistakenly attack nerve cells (Backert 2021).
In implementing a Food Safety Criterion under Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005, competent authorities must balance public-health gains with broader sustainability goals. A stricter FSC, if applied without proportionality or rapid testing support, risks leading to large-scale rejection of poultry batches that marginally exceed microbiological limits. Such outcomes would contradict the EU Green Deal and its Farm-to-Fork Strategy, which call for halving food waste by 2030, improving resource efficiency, and promoting sustainable food systems. Integrating risk-based sampling, predictive microbiological modeling, and dynamic shelf-life management would allow safety to be assured while reducing unnecessary disposal of food that remains safe for consumption. In the context of a growing population and climate-related constraints on arable land and water, regulators are ethically bound to adopt approaches that protect both public health and sustainable food availability.
. Ethical Foundation: Reducing Campylobacter infections is both a scientific and moral duty under the One Health framework, which recognizes the interconnection between human, animal, and environmental health. Public health authorities have an ethical responsibility to prevent avoidable harm by applying evidence-based measures that limit pathogen loads throughout the poultry chain. Following the Manhattan Principles, this approach calls for shared surveillance, collaboration across sectors, and preventive actions that protect public health while respecting animal welfare and environmental integrity. By enforcing hygiene standards, strengthening surveillance, and adopting rapid diagnostics, authorities act on a moral obligation to safeguard vulnerable populations and promote collective well-being, fairness, and sustainability. In doing so, regulators also emphasize transparency — openly communicating surveillance findings and their uncertainties to both industry risk managers and the public — to ensure that risk management decisions remain evidence-based and proportionate (Foddai et al., 2024; EFSA BIOHAZ Panel, 2011)..
