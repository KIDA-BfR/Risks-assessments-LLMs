Your description is as follows: Description: Food business operators are legally responsible for supplying safe food and must take measures when products are not safe according to legislation. Under EU microbiological criteria regulation (Commission Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005), poultry slaughterhouses implement routine Campylobacter testing to monitor compliance, with any failures requiring immediate corrective actions (Foddai et al., 2024). However, the Campylobacter problem is an ongoing operational and economic challenge shaped by the difficulty of controlling an organism that is both a natural, asymptomatic commensal in livestock — particularly poultry (Balta et al., 2022) — and a significant pathogen for humans (Zainol et al., 2024). The bacterium colonizes the intestinal tract of poultry at extremely high levels (Hermans et al., 2012), often reaching up to 10^9 CFU per gram (Abd El-Hack et al., 2021), without causing any clinical signs of disease (Humphrey et al., 2014). This creates a large, silent reservoir at the primary production level (Sadek et al., 2023). Flocks are frequently contaminated through horizontal transmission from the farm environment (Agunos et al., 2014), with key risk factors including insufficient cleaning and disinfection of barns (Gibbens et al., 2001), the presence of pests such as flies and rodents (Szott & Friese, 2021), contaminated water sources (Newell et al., 2011), and mechanical carriage via personnel, clothing, and equipment (Ridley et al., 2011). The slaughterhouse represents a critical point for the amplification and spread of contamination (Soro et al., 2020). Evidence shows that a Campylobacter-colonized broiler flock is approximately 30 times more likely to result in contaminated carcasses compared to a non-colonized flock (EFSA, 2010b). High-risk stages for cross-contamination include scalding (Yang & Johnson, 2001), defeathering, and evisceration (Seliwiorstow et al., 2016), where bacteria can be transferred between carcasses and processing equipment (Slader et al., 2002). Consequently, producers face the dual pressure of making substantial investments in on-farm biosecurity, hygiene, and process control (Alter & Reich, 2021) to meet stringent regulatory standards (Anonymous, 2017a), while also managing the economic risks and potential reputational damage associated with non-compliance with microbiological criteria (Pitter et al., 2018).
Transitioning from a Process Hygiene Criterion to a Food Safety Criterion will likely increase compliance costs and may cause significant food losses if entire batches are withdrawn post-production. The industry emphasizes that such losses conflict with the EU Farm-to-Fork Strategy’s circular-economy goals and the Green Deal’s climate-neutral ambition. Consequently, food business operators advocate integrating real-time monitoring, predictive risk algorithms, and in-line testing to intervene early — before packaging — thereby minimizing waste and preserving resources while still meeting safety targets.

Additionally, strict adherence to operational hygiene best practices at critical processing steps is essential, as studies show that slaughterhouse hygiene scores correlate with final carcass contamination levels (Habib et al., 2012). Adopt and validate novel physical and chemical decontamination technologies — such as cold plasma, UV light, pulsed electric fields, and peracetic acid or plant-derived antimicrobial rinses — to achieve 1–3 log reductions without compromising product quality. Combine these with modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) and antimicrobial films to inhibit post-processing bacterial survival and maintain product safety through distribution.

General aim: To reduce the Campylobacter burden in poultry and the corresponding risk to consumers by applying a multi-hurdle control framework that combines improved biosecurity management, microbiota-based pre-harvest interventions, and novel in-plant and packaging technologies validated through quantitative risk assessment.. Scientific Foundation: The industry’s focus on targeted on-farm and in-plant interventions is well supported by evidence identifying critical control points in the poultry chain (Alter & Reich, 2021). Contaminated barn environments and poor biosecurity remain major risk factors for flock infection (Agunos et al., 2014). Measures such as hygiene barriers and fly screens (Hald et al., 2007), and controlled access, effectively reduce Campylobacter colonization on farms (Newell et al., 2011). Pre-harvest interventions, including probiotics like Lactobacillus and Bacillus strains, further lower intestinal Campylobacter loads in live birds (Balta et al., 2022).
At processing, innovative physical technologies such as cold plasma, UV light, and pulsed electric fields achieve 1–3 log reductions on carcass surfaces without heat damage or chemical residues (Soro et al., 2020). Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) with CO₂-rich environments slows bacterial survival post-processing (Meredith et al., 2014), especially when combined with antimicrobial films or plasma treatment (Soro et al., 2020). Peracetic acid and plant-derived antimicrobials provide similar reductions when used as rinses or sprays (Nagel et al., 2013). Additionally, phytochemicals such as carvacrol, citrus peel extracts, and β-resorcylic acid inhibit motility, adhesion, and biofilm formation (Abd El-Hack et al., 2021), with in vivo studies reporting 1.7–2.5 log Campylobacter cecal reductions (Wagle et al., 2017). Together, these multi-hurdle interventions offer a robust, science-based approach to reducing Campylobacter contamination throughout the poultry production continuum (Alter & Reich, 2021).
Notably, the reliability of detection methods can influence control outcomes: different carcass sampling techniques vary in their ability to detect Campylobacter, which in turn affects surveillance results and compliance decisions (Ellis-Iversen et al., 2020). In addition, extensive economic analyses have been conducted to weigh the costs and benefits of various interventions. European studies (Mangen et al., 2007; Havelaar et al., 2007; Katsma et al., 2007) evaluated the cost-effectiveness of control measures, and their findings — summarized by Pitter et al. (2018) — help the industry prioritize interventions that maximize public health impact for a given cost.
. Ethical Foundation: The food industry has a clear ethical duty of care to prevent avoidable harm from Campylobacter along the poultry chain. Under a One Health perspective — which recognizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health — the food industry shares responsibility with public authorities to reduce zoonotic risk at its source and across processing, because poultry meat is a leading vehicle of human campylobacteriosis and even low infectious doses can cause disease. This duty is reinforced by the Manhattan Principles’ call for cross-sector collaboration, investment in surveillance, and preventive action to protect public health and ecosystem integrity.
The moral imperative is heightened by the substantial population burden and economic cost of disease in Europe: Campylobacter is among the most common food-borne bacterial infections, with large health-system and productivity losses — estimated at billions of euros annually — which justifies rigorous, risk-based hygiene controls by those who profit from poultry production. Practically, this ethical duty requires food businesses to:
●	Prevent and minimize contamination at its source through robust biosecurity and hygiene, acknowledging that birds can carry Campylobacter asymptomatically and that farm contamination drives risk downstream.
●	Control cross-contamination at slaughter, prioritizing the known high-risk steps (e.g., scalding, defeathering, evisceration) where decisions and investments by operators materially affect public risk.
●	Participate in harmonized monitoring and transparent reporting, supporting EU obligations for zoonoses data collection and risk management, and enabling timely corrective action.
●	Continuously improve by adopting validated interventions and rapid diagnostics that reduce pathogen loads without compromising animal welfare or the environment, consistent with One Health stewardship.
In summary, the industry’s social license to operate entails more than regulatory compliance: it is an ethical commitment to protect consumers and communities by systematically lowering Campylobacter risks across farming and processing, collaborating with regulators and suppliers, and investing in measures proportionate to the demonstrated public-health burden.
