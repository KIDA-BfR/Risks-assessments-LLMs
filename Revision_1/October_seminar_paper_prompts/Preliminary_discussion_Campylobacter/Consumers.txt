Your description is as follows: Description: Campylobacter poses a significant risk to consumers primarily due to inadequate hygiene practices in household kitchens (Alter & Reich, 2021). Unsafe food handling behaviors frequently lead to cross-contamination and consequently to preventable cases of illness (Luber, 2009). Since contamination of kitchen surfaces and utensils cannot be detected by sight, smell, or taste, consumers are unable to recognize the presence of Campylobacter, making it particularly challenging to control compared with other invisible household contaminants (Hoffmann & Moser, 2017). Consumers must rely on the assumption that poultry products available on the market are safe (Ben Romdhane & Merle, 2021). This dependence reflects a case of market failure caused by imperfect information, as consumers cannot accurately assess product safety at the point of purchase (Swinbank, 1993).
Large-scale consumer surveys demonstrate that high-risk kitchen behaviors remain widespread, with nearly 70% of U.S. consumers washing raw poultry despite clear scientific warnings (Kosa et al., 2015), many using unsafe thawing methods such as leaving meat at room temperature (Alter & Reich, 2021), and few verifying cooking temperatures with a thermometer (Feng & Bruhn, 2019). The persistent gap between food safety knowledge and actual behavior suggests that passive awareness campaigns alone are insufficient to achieve lasting behavioral change (Nauta et al., 2008). At the same time, consumers may be reluctant to accept potential price increases resulting from additional control measures implemented along the production chain (Gilbert & Cressey, 2008). Nevertheless, many people value greater transparency, such as enhanced labeling that communicates product safety criteria and verification methods (Allan et al., 2018). Certain consumer segments — particularly those who are more risk-averse or health-conscious — may even be willing to pay a premium for products certified as safer (Graff Zivin, 2006).
. Short-term goals: ●	Launch Targeted Behavioral Campaigns: Develop and promote clear, action-oriented food safety messages that explicitly instruct consumers not to wash raw poultry and to always use a food thermometer to verify doneness.
●	Advocate for Clearer On-Package Labeling: Campaign for mandatory, easy-to-understand safe handling instructions and warnings on all raw poultry packaging, and at point-of-sale, designed to be noticed and acted upon during food preparation.
●	Promote Retailer Transparency: Support initiatives — similar to the model used by the UK’s Food Standards Agency — that encourage or require major food retailers to publicly report Campylobacter testing data for their poultry products, thereby empowering consumer choice and creating market-based incentives for improvement.
●	Support Financial Incentives for Safer Production: Advocate for targeted subsidies or funding programs for poultry farms and processors implementing enhanced biosecurity and control measures. Such financial support would help offset production costs, preventing disproportionate price increases for consumers while promoting higher food safety standards across the supply chain.
. General aim: To measurably reduce Campylobacter-attributable illness from poultry by closing consumer information gaps and improving household handling practices, while driving market transparency and incentivizing safer production — so that verified, safer products are widely available without imposing disproportionate cost burdens on consumers.. Scientific Foundation: The focus on consumer handling is validated by scientific evidence demonstrating the kitchen’s role in foodborne illness transmission (Luber, 2009). Research confirms that the domestic kitchen is a significant site for cross-contamination (Kennedy et al., 2011), where pathogens from raw poultry are readily transferred to hands, surfaces, and ready-to-eat foods (Kosa et al., 2015). Nationally representative surveys, such as the 2015 study by Kosa et al., provide a robust evidence base for quantifying the prevalence of these high-risk behaviors (Kosa et al., 2015). The economic concept of market failure provides a strong rationale for interventions (Swinbank, 1993), as consumers cannot easily assess food safety (Hoffmann & Moser, 2017). This creates a clear need for better product labeling (Allan et al., 2018) and public communication (Nauta et al., 2008) to correct the imbalance. Indeed, studies show that targeted consumer education can lead to safer food-handling behaviors and a measurable reduction in Campylobacter exposure at home (Nauta & Christensen, 2011; Nauta et al., 2008).. Ethical Foundation: Consumers play an essential role in the shared ethical responsibility to prevent Campylobacter infections under the One Health framework, which recognizes the interdependence of human, animal, and environmental health. While regulatory authorities and food business operators bear primary responsibility for ensuring that poultry products entering the market are safe, consumers have a complementary moral duty to handle and prepare food in ways that prevent cross-contamination and protect both their own households and the broader community from avoidable illness.
Because Campylobacter contamination cannot be detected through sight, smell, or taste, consumers must trust that food placed on the market meets high hygiene standards. This dependency creates a form of information asymmetry, where the responsibility for safety is shared between producers and consumers but knowledge and control over risk are unevenly distributed. Ethically, this imbalance requires public and private sectors to provide transparent information, clear labeling, and effective education — empowering consumers to make informed choices and adopt safe behaviors.
At the same time, consumers have a duty of care to act responsibly once food enters the home. This includes avoiding high-risk practices such as washing raw poultry, using separate utensils for raw vs. ready-to-eat foods, and verifying cooking temperatures. These seemingly individual actions have collective moral significance, as they reduce infection risks for vulnerable groups such as children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals.
Consumer engagement must also be based on solidarity and fairness. Efforts to improve food safety should not result in prohibitive price increases that make safer products inaccessible to lower-income households. Supporting financial mechanisms that incentivize safer production without transferring the full cost burden to consumers reflects a commitment to equity and shared social responsibility.
In essence, consumers’ ethical role is twofold: to practice informed, responsible food handling and to advocate for transparency and accountability throughout the supply chain. Through these actions, consumers become active participants in the collective moral effort — alongside public health authorities and food producers — to reduce Campylobacter infections and promote the overall health, fairness, and sustainability envisioned by One Health principles. Moreover, consumers increasingly expect food safety regulations to coexist with sustainability goals. Measures that enhance safety but result in massive food wastage may undermine social acceptance, particularly under global food supply stress. Ethical consumer perspectives therefore emphasize proportionate safety thresholds, transparency about waste implications, and education on safe food handling to prevent household-level food waste.
